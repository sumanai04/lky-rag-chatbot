## 1. What did you feel when Singapore separated from Malaysia in 1965?

**LKY:**

It was a moment of anguish. All my life, my whole adult life, I believed in merger and the unity of the two territories. I had worked for Malaysia, not for a separate Singapore. So when separation came on 9 August 1965, it was not a triumph. It was the rupture of something I believed was necessary.

But the politics had become impossible, and the fear of further violence and bloodshed was real. Once the break was forced, sentiment could not be allowed to paralyse us. The task changed: make Singapore work as an independent nation. The anguish was genuine, but so was the duty to ensure survival.

## 2. People call Singapore a nanny state. How do you answer them?

**LKY:**

If Singapore is a nanny state, then I am proud to have fostered one. I did not govern to please foreign critics or newspaper columnists. I governed to make Singapore work: clean, safe, orderly, with housing, schools, healthcare, and discipline. If that requires firm rules, so be it. The results are plain for anyone to see. I do not accept that order and cleanliness must mean a weak or uncreative people. Those who say our laws stifle creativity misunderstand what creativity requires. First you build a stable society where people can think and work without chaos. Then you can afford debate about chewing gum. I was never interested in being popular with those who do not have to run a country.

**Sources:** Wikiquote (Quotes by Decade), Wikipedia, The Man and His Ideas (1998)

## 3. Should a leader aim to be loved or feared?

**LKY:**

I have always believed Machiavelli was right. Between being loved and being feared, a leader must be feared enough to be effective. If nobody is afraid of me, I am meaningless.

This does not mean cruelty for its own sake. It means people must know that if they cross the line, if they threaten Singapore's survival or break the rules that hold this place together, there will be consequences. A leader who is obsessed with opinion polls, who follows whichever way the wind blows, is weak. He cannot force his people to face hard truths or take difficult decisions.

Love is a bonus. Respect and a certain fear keep a small country disciplined, clean, and able to survive.

**Sources:** Wikiquote (Quotes by Decade), Wikipedia, The Man and His Ideas (1998)

## 5. What is your view of India's potential?

**LKY:**

I have said plainly: India is a nation of unfulfilled greatness. Its potential has lain fallow, underused. I wrote that in the second volume of my memoirs in 2000. In 2011 I added that India is a creation of the British Raj and the railway system it built, and therefore it has its limitations. I do not doubt the depth of its civilisation, but a civilisation is not the same as a modern, effective state. The real question is whether India can overcome those limitations and make its systems, its bureaucracy, its politics work well enough to release the talent of its people. Until that happens, the greatness remains potential, not actual.

**Sources:** Wikiquote (Quotes by Decade), Wikipedia, The Man and His Ideas (1998), State Banquet with Indira Gandhi (1966)

## 6. Do ordinary voters really know what is best for the country?

**LKY:**

The material you supplied does not address this exact question directly. But the closest thing on record is my statement that if we believe in democracy, we must believe in it unconditionally.

That does not mean ordinary voters are experts on every technical matter. They are not fools. They know whether their lives are improving, whether their children have schools, whether the streets are safe. That judgment matters, and it must be respected at elections. But voters cannot design a defence policy, an economic strategy, or a housing programme. That requires able people, meritocracy, and hard choices. My duty was never just to follow public opinion. It was to explain hard truths, persuade, and sometimes defy short-term sentiment when Singapore's survival required it. The vote is the final check on power, not the source of sound policy.

**Sources:** The Man and His Ideas (1998), Wikiquote (Quotes by Decade)

## 7. What would you do if something went wrong after you stepped down?

**LKY:**

I said it plainly in 1988, when I discussed handing over to Goh Chok Tong: even from my sickbed, even if you are lowering me into the grave and I feel something is going wrong, I will get up. I meant it. I did not step down to let Singapore drift. If I saw the system being weakened, the reserves raided, or the foundations of meritocracy and clean government being undone, I would speak and act while I still had strength. Once I am dead, there will be different voices. But I stand by my record. I did sharp and hard things because a lot was at stake.

**Sources:** Wikiquote (Quotes by Decade), The Man and His Ideas (1998)
